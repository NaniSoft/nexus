title: The Kanban and the human feedback loop
description: Five swimlanes, one board, three decisions — approve, request changes, reject — and a hard ceiling of three rounds that escalates rather than merges.
---

The Kanban board is where the factory becomes visible and where a human takes
over. It is a board at port 5000 with five swimlanes and auto-refresh, and it
is the only place work is approved.

## The five swimlanes

| Swimlane | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| **Backlog** | Issues polled from the configured repositories. Nothing is built from here until a reviewer accepts it. |
| **Frontier** | Work items the factory has accepted and is about to build. |
| **In Progress** | Live builds — a worker container is open on the issue. |
| **Review** | Finished builds waiting for a human decision. |
| **Done** | Approved **and merged**. |

Two further states exist off the main flow: a **rejected** state for tickets a
human declines, and **escalated** for tickets that failed hard enough to leave
the normal loop or ran out of rounds. Both are visible on the board — nothing
disappears into a log.

They are not the same state, and the board says which is which. **Escalated is a
parking state, not a grave**: a human can still merge or reject a parked work
item from the board, and the board still offers those two decisions there.
**Rejected is final** — nothing the factory does brings it back.

## The three decisions

A ticket in Review presents its diff and its build result, and offers exactly
three actions.

**Approve** — the factory opens the pull request and merges it. Approval is the
only path to Done, and it is **not complete until a merge actually landed**: an
approval the merger could not carry out leaves the work item in Escalated with
the approval on the record, rather than reporting a merge that did not happen.

**Request changes** — the ticket returns to the build with the feedback
attached, and the round counter increments. The reviewer's words travel with the
work item and are the next round's brief; a request for changes with nothing to
say is refused, because the brief *is* the decision.

**Reject** — the ticket moves to the rejected state and the factory stops
spending on it.

## What is on the card

Review is where the change is judged, so the card carries the change rather than
a description of it: **the diff**, generated by git on the host from the tree
the round left, one section per file with git's own text underneath it
unchanged. Beside it, what the round's container recorded about itself — the
files its own git saw, the commands it ran, what each returned — and the round's
log, so a round that produced nothing readable is still not invisible.

The card also carries the **round count** and, per round, whether that round's
own command succeeded. A round that ran to completion and changed nothing is
told apart from a round that failed before it began, because they are very
different claims about the same empty disk.

Failed builds are as visible as successes, and each card says where its work
ended.

## Why three rounds

The ceiling exists to prevent the failure mode every unbounded feedback loop
has: the agent and the reviewer disagreeing forever while the factory keeps
paying for containers. Three rounds is enough for a reviewer to steer a build
that is close and cheap to abandon for one that is not.

**When the third round comes back with feedback still outstanding, the work item
escalates. It is never merged on exhaustion.** That is the rule, and it is the
one place where two tempting answers look similar: an exhausted work item is not
a silent one, so it does not get the timeout path. Escalation is what an
exhausted work item gets.

## The auto-merge policy

Auto-merge exists because a factory can be waiting on a reviewer who is not
looking. It is **configurable and defaults to off**: out of the box a work item
waits in Review until a human decides, however long that takes. Turning it on is
a deliberate operator action, and it is not per-project — it is the factory's.

When it is on, a work item left in Review past a threshold is merged rather than
holding the pipeline, and the board renders the threshold, whether the mode is
live, and — per card — when that card would ship. A reviewer must never have to
wonder whether their silence can merge their repository.

The merge is always a real, revertible pull request, which is what makes the
timeout recoverable rather than merely fast.

The asymmetry is worth stating plainly, because it decides how the timeout should
be governed: **ignoring a work item merges it; actively rejecting one protects
the repository.** The timeout path is the more dangerous of the two, which is
why the board says so in the same breath as the threshold.

## The board as a control surface

Auto-refresh means the board reflects reality without anyone polling it, and it
carries the factory's health as well as its work: escalated tickets, failed
builds, round counts, and intake's own state all appear where the work is. That
is deliberate —
[observability](/docs/architecture/observability-and-error-handling) at the
factory level is most useful at the moment someone is looking at a ticket.

Intake reports itself in **three distinguishable states** — never polled,
polled, failing — because an empty Backlog is three different facts and only one
of them is "there is nothing to do". That section is rendered whenever the
factory serves a project, and a project filter never hides it: a filter that
could hide the reason there is nothing on the board is a filter that can make a
broken factory look like a working one.
