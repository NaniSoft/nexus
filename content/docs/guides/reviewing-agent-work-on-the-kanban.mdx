---
title: Reviewing agent work on the Kanban
description: The three decisions, what to read before making one, and how the three-round ceiling and the auto-merge setting change the conversation.
---

Reviewing is the factory's human job. A build arrives in **Review** with its
diff and its build results, and you make one of three decisions. Everything else
about the role follows from taking that seriously.

## What is in front of you

A Review ticket carries what happened, not a summary of it.

**The diff**, as git wrote it, generated on the host from the tree the round
left. One section per file, the first open and the rest folded, with git's own
text underneath each one **unchanged** — so you can hold the card against your own
`git diff` and get the same bytes. Beside it, what the round's container recorded
about itself: the files its own git saw, the commands it ran, what each returned,
and the round's log if it produced nothing readable.

Two things on the card are worth reading deliberately, because a card that
distinguishes them is telling you something a card that does not is not:

- **"the round ran to completion and changed nothing"** versus **"the round's own
  command did not succeed"**. Same untouched disk, opposite claims about the
  round — the first is a real outcome, the second is a round that stopped.
- **How much was left off** when the change was very large. A diff that stops
  without saying how much it stopped at is indistinguishable from a diff of a
  smaller change, so the card counts the files and lines it did not render and
  says where the whole of it is on this machine.

Read the tests first. The factory runs the project's suite, so a green run is
evidence, but it is evidence about what the project already tests. A change with
no test touching it is unverified no matter how green the run is.

Then read the diff as a whole. Agent work fails in characteristic ways — a change
that solves the issue in one place while quietly breaking an assumption somewhere
else, or a fix that adds a special case where the codebase already has an
abstraction. Whole-diff reading is what catches those.

## The three decisions

**Approve** merges. Do it when the diff answers the issue and you would have
merged it from a colleague. Approval is a signature on the change, not a
thank-you to the factory — and it is not complete until a merge actually lands.
If the merger could not carry it out, the ticket ends up in Escalated with your
approval on the record, which is the honest answer rather than a Done that
happened to nobody.

**Request changes** sends the ticket back with your feedback attached and
consumes one of the three rounds. **Write the feedback as you would for a
colleague's pull request**: specific, actionable, and pointed at the change
rather than the agent. "This is wrong" costs a round and produces another guess;
"this path drops the error case — the caller in X assumes it is set" produces a
fix. Your words are the next round's brief, which is why a request for changes
with nothing to say is refused: the brief *is* the decision.

**Reject** ends the ticket. Use it when the issue itself turns out to be wrong,
duplicated, or not worth the remaining rounds. Rejection is not a judgement on
the build; it is the decision that the work should not continue, and it is
**final** — nothing the factory does brings it back.

## Three rounds, so spend them well

The [round ceiling](/docs/concepts/kanban-and-the-human-feedback-loop#why-three-rounds)
exists so that disagreement terminates. It also changes what good feedback
looks like: three rounds is enough to steer a build that is close, and not
enough to design something by committee through the board.

When the third round comes back, the ticket **escalates** — it parks for a human
rather than merging. Escalation is not a dead end: the board still offers you
approve and reject there, and those are the only two ways out.

If you are asking for changes a second time, stop and consider whether the issue
was buildable. If it was not, the honest move is
[reject and write a better issue](/docs/guides/writing-an-issue-the-factory-can-build#the-ceiling-is-a-feature)
rather than a third round aimed at a target that keeps moving.

## The auto-merge threshold

**Out of the box, a ticket in Review waits for you however long it takes.**
Auto-merge is a factory-level setting and its default is **off**, so nothing ships
unattended until an operator deliberately turns it on.

When it is on, a ticket left in Review past the threshold is merged rather than
holding the pipeline, and the board shows the threshold, shows that the mode is
live, and puts the moment *that card* would ship on the card itself. Read that
before you rely on it: Review is a place where work is waiting for a decision,
not a place where it is safely parked.

The asymmetry is worth carrying with you: **ignoring a work item merges it, while
actively rejecting one protects the repository.** The timeout path is the more
dangerous of the two, which is why the board says so beside the threshold.
