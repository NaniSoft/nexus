An unbounded feedback loop is the failure mode nobody designs on purpose and
every automated system finds anyway. The agent and the reviewer disagree; the
ticket goes back; the agent changes something; the reviewer asks again. Each
cycle costs a container, a build, and a human read — and none of them is ever
the last one.

Nexus closes the loop. This post is about the two rules that close it, and the
one that makes them bearable.

## Three decisions, not one button

A build lands in **Review** with its diff and its build results, and the board
offers exactly three actions.

**Approve** opens the pull request and merges it. It is the only path to Done.

**Request changes** returns the ticket to the build with your feedback
attached, and consumes one of the three rounds.

**Reject** ends the ticket in a rejected state and stops the factory spending
on it.

The third decision is the one most review tools leave out, and leaving it out
is a mistake. A factory with only "approve" and "more changes" forces a human
to keep steering work that should never have started — an issue that turned out
to be wrong, duplicated, or not worth the remaining rounds. Rejection is not a
judgement on the build. It is the decision that the work should stop, and it is
a normal outcome rather than a failure one.

That is also why both states off the flow are on the board. Rejected and
escalated tickets are rendered where everyone looks, because a queue that hides
its failures trains the people watching it to stop trusting it — and because
escalated is not a dead end: it parks for a human, and the board still offers
approve and reject there.

## Why three rounds

Three is not a magic number; it is the number at which the conversation
changes character.

One round is steering: "you missed the error path", "keep the API unchanged".
It is the normal cost of working with a colleague you cannot talk to
mid-change, and it is usually worth it.

Two rounds is design disagreement: the reviewer wants a different shape and the
build keeps producing this one. Still recoverable, but now the missing
conversation is a design conversation, and the board is a bad place to have it.

Three rounds and still going means the requirement was never understood — by
the person writing the issue, or by the person reading it. The factory stops
there, and the honest response is to close the ticket and write a better issue.
That is why the ceiling is a ceiling: not to punish the agents, but to force
the fourth iteration to happen in the specification, where it is cheap, rather
than in the factory, where it is not.

There is a cost side to this too. Every round is a real container, a real
build, a real model spend. A factory without a ceiling does not fail loudly; it
just gets slowly more expensive while producing nothing anyone merges.

## On exhaustion, escalate rather than merge

Three rounds and the feedback is still outstanding is not a ticket that should
be merged, and it is worth being exact about why the loop **escalates** there
rather than falling through to the timeout path.

A work item that has been called wrong three times is one a human actively
disagrees with. Merging it over that objection would defeat the only guarantee
the design makes, and it would do so quietly — the change would look merged, the
round count would look spent, and nothing on the board would say that a reviewer
had said no. So exhaustion parks the ticket in **Escalated**, which is a state a
human finishes from the board: approve or reject are still offered there, and
those are the only two ways out of it. Rejected is the state that is final.

That is the asymmetry the whole design turns on: **ignoring a work item merges
it; actively rejecting one protects the repository.** Escalation is what keeps
the two apart.

## Auto-merge on timeout, and why it is off by default

The factory merges on **approval**, and it *can* merge on **feedback timeout** —
a ticket left in Review past the threshold merging rather than holding the
pipeline.

It ships **off**. Out of the box a work item waits in Review until a human
decides, however long that takes, and turning the timeout on is a deliberate
operator action. The board says which mode is live, states the threshold, and
puts the moment each individual card would ship on the card itself — a reviewer
must never have to wonder whether their silence can merge their repository.

The reasoning for having the mode at all is worth keeping, because it is the
reason the default is a setting rather than the reason the setting is off.

A factory's throughput is bounded by its review queue, and its review queue is
bounded by people who have other work. That is fine until it is not: a queue
nobody reads is indistinguishable from a factory that has stopped, except that
the factory keeps paying. Auto-merge on timeout is the design choosing the
failure it prefers. Holding the pipeline silently is a failure nobody notices
until someone asks why nothing has shipped in three weeks; merging a revertible
pull request is a failure that is visible, bounded, and one `git revert` from
gone.

It is also worth being precise about what is being trusted. The factory is not
deciding that the code is good. It is deciding that the work should not wait
indefinitely for a decision that was available and not made. The review
opportunity was real, it was visible on a board with auto-refresh, and it
expired.

And the merge is always a pull request — reviewable, revertible, attributed. A
factory that merged directly to a branch would be taking a different kind of
liberty entirely.

## What makes the loop good, in practice

The rules only work if the input does. A round spent on "what did you mean by
this?" is a wasted container, and the fix for that is upstream of the factory:
[an issue a competent engineer could act on without asking you
anything](/docs/guides/writing-an-issue-the-factory-can-build).

Which is the quiet conclusion of the whole design. The factory does not make
review cheaper by automating it away. It makes review rarer by making the work
arrive in a state where one round of it is usually enough — and by refusing to
run forever when it is not.
