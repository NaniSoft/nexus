There are five nanisoft.com sites. This one, Atlas, AlphaLens, Prism, and the
company root. They share one design language, one component library, one
pack spectrum, and one chrome — and every one of them was built by an agent
working a repository with a person directing it.

Not by Nexus. Nexus is not shipping yet. That distinction is the whole point of
this post.

## What actually happened

Each site went the way agent development goes today: a person opened a
repository, pointed a coding agent at a spec, reviewed what came back, asked
for changes, and eventually merged. Repeat per site, per feature, per fix.

The outputs are good, and they are real — you are reading one. But the process
was bespoke every time. The environment was whatever the person's machine had.
The specification was a prompt written for one session. The review was a
diff in a terminal. Nothing about it compounds: the second site did not get
cheaper because the first one happened, except in the ways the person got
better at directing.

That is the difference between a capability and a factory. The capability is
here. The factory is what Nexus is for.

## What the factory changes

Take the same work and put it through the designed loop, and the parts that
were per-session become per-project configuration:

- **The environment** becomes a container from a named image — identical every
  run, torn down after, unable to reach another project's work.
- **The specification** becomes a GitHub issue, the only input the factory
  reads. No prompt to tune per session; the issue is the spec.
- **The review** becomes a board with three decisions and a ceiling: approve,
  request changes, reject, three rounds maximum.
- **The record** becomes the factory's own. Structured logs, metrics,
  escalation. A team can inspect what happened instead of asking the person who
  was there.

The first three are exactly the steps that were manual every time. That is not
a coincidence — it is the definition of the work.

## The platform story, and its honest tense

NaniSoft's story is that Nexus is the core, and the products are built on it:
Atlas modelling real systems, AlphaLens for quantitative research, Prism as the
design language they all wear. You will read that on every one of these sites,
because it is the architecture we are building toward.

Read it in the right tense. Today, agent development built these sites, and a
person held the loop. The design in these docs is what makes that repeatable
without the person in the middle of every step — one factory, many projects,
round-robin across a directory of YAML files, a human review that terminates.

## Why publish the design first

Because the design is the part that benefits from being argued about before it
is code, and the release is the part that does not.

Everything Nexus claims is written down where it can be checked: the
[architecture](/docs/architecture/system-overview), the
[configuration model](/docs/configuration/the-configuration-model), the
[testing strategy](/docs/operations/testing-strategy), and the two rules this
post leans on — [three rounds, and merge on
timeout](/blog/humans-in-the-loop-three-rounds-max). There are no screenshots
here, no quickstart, and no changelog, because there is nothing to screenshot,
install, or release.

The next thing Nexus builds should be built through the factory: an issue on a
configured repository, a fresh container, a diff on a board, a person deciding.
That is the milestone this site is waiting to announce — and when it happens,
it will be the first claim on this site that is about Nexus rather than about
the design of it.
